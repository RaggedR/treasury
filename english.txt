THE TREASURY — what we are modelling, in plain English
Version 0.1 — 2026-10-29

Picture a company's books. The state here is a chart of accounts — a tree of
accounts like cash, accounts-payable, sales, wages — each holding a balance. The
world is those balances, and the one iron rule is double-entry. Money never
appears or vanishes, it only moves, so every change touches two accounts at once
and the two sides must be equal. A posting that does not balance is not a
mistake to be corrected later — it is simply not a legal thing to do, and the
books refuse it. Every posting that does go through is written into the journal,
which is append-only. You never erase a journal entry. If something was wrong,
you post a new reversing entry, and the mistake and its correction both stay on
the record forever.

Four people work the books, and again each is allowed to touch only their own
corner.

  - The auditor can read but never post. They can pull any account's balance,
    run a trial balance to prove the whole thing still sums to zero, and read
    the journal. Their job is to check, not to change.

  - The payables clerk records what the firm owes and spends. They can post, but
    only against the liability and expense accounts — the money-going-out side.

  - The receivables clerk records what the firm is owed and earns. They can post
    too, but only against the asset and income accounts — the money-coming-in
    side. Notice that the clerks have the same command, post, but it is fenced
    to different accounts for each of them. The permission is not only which
    command but which accounts it may touch.

  - The controller posts nothing routine. The controller delegates — hands a
    stack of invoices to receivables and a stack of bills to payables — and then
    does the thing only they may do, which is to open new accounts, reverse bad
    entries, and close the period. To close the period they add up the
    sub-accounts into their parents, all the way up the tree, and check that the
    whole lot still balances.

Now a scene. A batch of customer invoices arrives. The controller tells
receivables "book these," and receivables posts each one as a debit to
accounts-receivable and a credit to sales, with a reason like "invoice 1182,
services rendered to Acme in October." Each posting balances, so each one
commits and lands in the journal. Meanwhile a bill comes in that someone has
keyed wrong, with the debit and credit not equal. The books refuse it outright —
it never enters the journal, because an unbalanced posting is not a legal move.
At period end the controller rolls every sub-account up into its parent, the
balances sum, the trial balance comes out to zero, and the auditor independently
confirms the books close.

And this scales up. A parent company consolidates the ledgers of its
subsidiaries. The parent never sees each subsidiary's individual accounts — it
only receives one consolidated balance from each, rolled up and handed upward. A
group's books are just ledgers nested inside a ledger.

The shape underneath. There is a world of state, a set of workers who may each
touch only their own slice of it, a boss who gathers their reports and issues
the orders that actually change things, a hard rule that quietly refuses
anything illegal, and a permanent record of every change and the reason for it.
The treasury is the version that makes the adding-up honest, because here the
reports really do sum. Its sibling, the starship, is the version that makes the
delegation and the who-may-touch-what vivid. See the sibling showpiece at
https://github.com/RaggedR/starship.

The shape itself — a worker as a container morphism, the boss who gathers and
decides, the gate that quietly refuses anything that does not balance — is
MacBeth's. It is set down in Note 5, "The toy harness is a worker"
(~/git/harness/toy-harness-is-a-worker.pdf), which MacBeth sent in
~/git/harness/2026-10-11-macbeth-reply-toy-harness-is-a-worker.txt.
